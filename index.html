One project I’m proud of is Huntech, a New Zealand outdoor retailer specialising in technical hunting gear. Their eCommerce experience was no longer keeping up with the needs of the business, and there were several opportunities to improve the shopping experience and conversion.

My role was to lead the UI redesign, working within a multidisciplinary team under our Design Lead.

Before designing, I did a heuristic analysis to find the biggest opportunities, so we focused on the PDP and PLP, where customers compare products and make purchase decisions.

I grouped the findings into three areas: Usability, Comprehension and Merchandising. From these, I identified five design opportunities: Product Confidence, Product Discovery, Mobile Filtering, Product Communication and Shopping Efficiency.

I also contributed to the Design System and prepared the handoff for the developers.

After launch, the agency reported a 125% increase in revenue and a 41% increase in Average Order Value. Many factors can influence these results, but the project showed me how good UI design can reduce uncertainty, improve the customer experience and support business goals.

Context. Huntech is a New Zealand outdoor retailer specialising in technical hunting gear. Their eCommerce experience was no longer keeping up with the needs of the business. The platform had several limitations that affected the shopping experience and created opportunities to improve conversion.

My role. I led the UI redesign, working within a multidisciplinary team under our Design Lead. I was responsible for identifying key usability issues, translating findings into practical UI solutions, contributing to the Design System and preparing the designs for developer handoff.

Audit & diagnosis. Before designing, I wanted to understand where we could create the biggest impact. I did a heuristic analysis across the purchase journey to find friction points affecting both the user experience and business goals. The review showed opportunities in Usability, Comprehension and Merchandising.

Rather than redesigning the whole website, we focused on the Product Listing Page and Product Detail Page, because that’s where customers compare products and make purchase decisions. The goal was to reduce purchase uncertainty and help users decide with more confidence.

Design decisions. I prioritised the highest-impact opportunities and turned them into five design decisions.

1. Product Confidence. Users couldn’t always see stock availability, sizing was inconsistent, and the Size Guide wasn’t very visible. I introduced real-time stock visibility, standard sizing patterns and a more visible Size Guide.

2. Product Discovery. The mobile navigation was unclear, search had no guidance, and the category structure was too text-heavy. I introduced a clearer navigation, guided search and visual category tiles to help users find products faster.

3. Mobile Filtering. Filtering needed multiple steps, there was no sorting option, and some touch targets were too small. I combined filtering and sorting into one flow and added larger, touch-safe controls.

4. Product Communication. Product benefits were mainly explained with text, and the Add to Cart button wasn’t always visible. I introduced visual icons for key benefits and a sticky Add to Cart button.

5. Shopping Efficiency. Users had to open the product page before adding an item to the cart, and the product cards were inconsistent. I introduced Quick Add from the listing page and made the product cards more consistent.

Design System & delivery. I also worked within the existing Design System, adapting and expanding components for Huntech’s needs. I documented the final designs in Figma and prepared them for developer handoff.

Impact. After launch, the agency reported a 125% increase in revenue and a 41% increase in Average Order Value. Many factors can influence business performance, but these results suggest that reducing uncertainty and improving the purchase experience can contribute to stronger commercial outcomes.

Reflection. The biggest lesson for me was that reducing uncertainty doesn’t always mean simplifying the interface. Users needed confidence that the size was available, the product was right for them, and it was worth the price. For me, this project showed how thoughtful UI design can support purchase decisions while balancing user needs and business goals.

Context (store availability, Blackpepper)→the limitation→what I did (adapt, ship a simpler version)→later outcome→how I work with devs

One example was at Blackpepper, on an eCommerce project. I designed a store availability experience for the product page, with Delivery, In Store and Click & Collect options.

During the technical review, the developer explained that the platform couldn’t support the original design at that stage, and we had a tight timeline.

So I adapted the design, and we shipped a simpler version that showed general availability instead of real-time store stock. It wasn’t the ideal experience, but it gave users the key information they needed.

Later, when the platform was updated, we were able to implement the original concept.

For me, it’s about talking to the developers early, looking at the designs together, being flexible and delivering the best possible product within the current constraints.

Si te repreguntan: What did the simplified version look like?

Instead of showing real-time stock for each store, we showed general availability. Users could see that the product was available, but they couldn’t select a specific store or see live stock.

Part of my workflow→colour and contrast→sizes and states→structure and content→handoff notes + example for screen readers→AI

Accessibility is part of my normal workflow, not just a final check.

I check that the colours have enough contrast, following WCAG. I also don’t use colour alone to show meaning. I look at the size of text, icons and buttons, the typography and the form states, like hover, error, success and disabled.

I also care about the structure and the content: a clear heading hierarchy, alt text and clear copy. I make sure the design works on different screen sizes and with zoom, and when there is motion, I follow standards like letting users pause or reduce it.

In the handoff, I leave notes for the developers, and sometimes examples or a working prototype. For screen readers, the code is what matters, so I work with the developers on it. For example, a button with only an icon needs a label, like “Add to cart”, so I add it in the handoff notes.

Today, AI tools also help me to find accessibility issues and fix them faster.

Si te preguntan por usuarios ciegos o sordos:

“I haven’t designed specifically for those users yet, but I know how it works. For blind users, a screen reader reads the code, so I would define the structure, the labels and the alt text, and work with the developers to build it. For deaf users, I would make sure the information doesn’t depend only on sound, with captions and visual alerts.”

Ayuda de memoria (para vos):
  • Un lector de pantalla (usado por personas ciegas) lee el código, no el diseño.
  • Vos definís: títulos, textos de botones, texto alternativo, orden de lectura. Lo dejás en las notas del handoff.
  • Los developers lo programan para que el lector lo entienda.
  • Ejemplo para recordar: un botón con solo ícono de carrito necesita la etiqueta “Add to cart”. Si no, el lector dice solo “botón”.
  • Sordos: subtítulos y que nada importante dependa solo del sonido.
